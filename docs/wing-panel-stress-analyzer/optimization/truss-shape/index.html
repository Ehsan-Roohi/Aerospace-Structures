Truss Shape Optimization: Moving the Nodes

truss-shape 0.1.1
Scope. Plane pin-jointed truss, linear elastic, small displacements, solved by the direct stiffness method. Node positions are the design variables; inside every geometry evaluation the member areas are sized by a fully stressed design loop against a stress check (allowable stress divided by the safety factor FS) and an Euler buckling screen for solid square sections (I = A2/12, effective length factor K as entered). Bracing wires are tension-only: a wire that would go into compression is released and flagged slack. Every optimum shown is re-solved from scratch with the exact solver before it is displayed. Nothing here is a certified strength, stability or fatigue statement, the material cards are educational values, and this page is independent of the Wing AeroStruct solver.

How it works

Nested approach: outer geometry, inner sizing
Students usually take the truss geometry as given and size the members. Here the outer design variables are node coordinates. For every candidate geometry the inner loop finds the lightest feasible set of member areas: starting from the preset areas, each sizing unit (a group or a single member) is updated with A ← max(Amin, A · max(Ustress, √Ubuckle)), where U is the utilization (demand divided by capacity, 1 means fully used). The square root appears because the Euler load of a solid square section grows with A2. The loop stops when every active unit has |U - 1| < 10-3 or after 40 iterations; a final uniform scaling guarantees U ≤ 1 exactly, and an optional displacement limit is met by scaling all areas uniformly (displacements scale exactly with 1/A). The mass of that sized design, in grams, is the outer objective. A geometry that is a mechanism gets a large finite penalty instead of NaN, so the optimizers simply move away from it.
Outer optimizers (deterministic, written from scratch)
Nelder-Mead simplex: n+1 trial geometries form a simplex that reflects, expands, contracts or shrinks on the normalized variables, with the adaptive coefficients of Gao and Han (expansion 1 + 2/n, contraction 0.75 - 1/(2n), shrink 1 - 1/n). Trial points are clamped to the bounds. Optional restarts rebuild the simplex around a slightly perturbed best point to escape a collapsed simplex.
CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy, (μ/μw, λ) form): each generation samples λ = 4 + ⌊3 ln n⌋ geometries from a Gaussian whose mean, step size and covariance adapt to the best μ = λ/2 samples (rank-one and rank-μ updates, cumulative step-size adaptation). Samples outside the bounds are evaluated at the clamped point plus a distance penalty. The random stream is the seeded mulberry32 generator of the lab, so a given seed always reproduces the same run. Both optimizers report the best geometry actually evaluated, never an interpolation.
Classical result: optimum height of a parallel-chord truss
Chord forces scale with the bending moment divided by the truss height h, so taller trusses have lighter chords. The diagonals get longer as h grows, their force per unit shear falls only slowly, and under an Euler screen a longer compression diagonal needs a much larger area (A ∝ L √N for a solid square). The sum of a falling chord mass and a rising web mass has an interior minimum. For stress-governed parallel-chord trusses the classical rule puts the optimum depth between about 1/8 and 1/5 of the span (0.125 to 0.2) with chord and web material of the same order; for buckling-governed designs the optimum is set by the slenderness of the compression diagonals, which depends on the panel length as much as on the height, so the optimum diagonal angle (about 45 to 60 degrees to the chord) is the more robust rule and the h/L value follows from the number of panels. The sweep plot shows the computed curve for this preset with the chord and web contributions, and the two optimizers are marked on it: an optimizer only finds what the full curve already shows, but it needs far fewer evaluations and scales to many variables, where a sweep is impossible. Toggle the buckling screen off to see the optimum move to a taller truss.
Limitations
Pin-jointed members, no joint bending, no self-weight, one load case at a time, no fatigue, no local or lateral-torsional buckling, no connection design. The Euler check is a screening indicator with an assumed effective length factor. Uniform scaling for the displacement limit is simple but not mass-optimal. Loads stay attached to their nodes, so when strut stations move the lift points move with them (total lift unchanged). Released wires are treated as absent; a bay whose two wires would both go slack keeps the less compressed wire active, because the spar bending stiffness that really holds such a bay is not in a pin-jointed model. The fully stressed design is the exact minimum for statically determinate trusses and a close, feasible design for indeterminate ones.
