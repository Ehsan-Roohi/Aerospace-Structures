7A-11. Real hardware: eight control mechanisms

Read one mechanism at a time. Each photograph is paired with a visual reading guide and a structural explanation. Click a photo to enlarge it. These examples include research UAVs, crewed test aircraft and a wind-tunnel model; their labels identify which is which. A photograph establishes visible hardware, not an entire flight-control law.

1. Elevon — one panel, two control roles

Photo reading: X-48B research UAV. The upright winglets are not elevons; look along the wing trailing edge below and inboard of them.

NASA / Carla Thomas. Photo source / credit. NASA X-48 fact sheet.

1. Locate the hardware. Find the rear edge of each swept wing. An elevon is a hinged portion of that edge, not the whole wing and not the vertical winglet. This oblique photograph locates the region; it does not resolve every hinge or commanded angle.

2. Describe the change. The left and right panels rotate about their hinges. Same-sense trailing-edge motion supplies a pitch-control channel; opposite-sense motion supplies a roll-control channel. The two requests can be added.

3. Explain the force and moment. Deflection changes local aerodynamic loading. For the generic aft-of-CG arrangement below, trailing edges up reduce upward loading and give a nose-up increment; unequal left/right increments give roll.

4. Follow the structural load. Panel pressure → hinge fittings AND actuator anchors → rear spar, ribs and wing skin.

Quick check: If both panels move up, why should you not call that command “right roll”?

2. Ruddervator — resolve the force on an inclined tail

Photo reading: MQ-9 Reaper. The two aft surfaces form a V when viewed from the front or rear. A separate ventral surface is also present.

U.S. Air Force / Staff Sgt. Brian Ferguson; public domain. Photo source / credit. USAF ruddervator reference, p. 16.

1. Locate the hardware. Look at the aft fuselage near the pusher propeller for the two inclined tail surfaces. The moving trailing-edge portion is the ruddervator; the fixed supporting tail surface is not itself the control panel.

2. Describe the change. A mixer combines pitch and yaw requests into two local panel deflections. Sketch each panel rotating relative to its own inclined tail, rather than describing both simply as “up” in the photograph.

3. Explain the force and moment. Resolve each local normal force into vertical and lateral components. Mirror-symmetric forces can add vertically and cancel laterally; a differential pattern can do the reverse. Exact signs and gains depend on geometry.

4. Follow the structural load. Hinge and actuator reactions → inclined tail spar/root fittings → rear fuselage. The fittings receive combined vertical and lateral loading.

Quick check: Why does one ruddervator reaching its travel limit affect both requested channels?

3. Spoileron — a spoiler used asymmetrically for roll

Photo reading: 737 spoiler hardware during descent. The raised panels are on the upper wing surface, ahead of the trailing edge.

Jg4817; CC BY-SA 3.0; existing course thumbnail. Photo source / credit. FAA Flight Controls, spoilers.

1. Locate the hardware. Identify the rectangular panel lifted above the wing and the dark gap beneath it. Do not confuse it with the trailing-edge flap or the winglet at the tip.

2. Describe the change. The panel rises into the airflow. “Spoiler” identifies the hardware; “spoileron” describes its asymmetric roll-control use. Raising both sides can instead provide speed-brake or lift-dump functions.

3. Explain the force and moment. Deploying a right-wing spoiler generally reduces right-wing lift and increases drag: right-wing-down roll and a nose-right yaw contribution under our body axes. The full response depends on the aircraft and other controls.

4. Follow the structural load. Pressure on the raised panel → hinge brackets AND actuator support → local ribs/spars/skin. Do not put all of the load into the actuator alone.

Quick check: This photo shows only one wing. Can it establish an asymmetric roll command? No: the opposite wing and command history are not shown.

4. Split drag rudder — opening a panel into two halves

Photo reading: Actual opened split surfaces on X-48C after landing. Look especially at the separated red upper and lower surfaces at image right.

NASA / Carla Thomas; ED12-0255-59. Photo source / credit. NASA X-36 directional-control example.

1. Locate the hardware. Unlike an ordinary single flap, the upper and lower portions separate like a clamshell. The gap and two distinct edges are the identifying visual cues.

2. Describe the change. Opening the halves exposes more area to the flow. NASA identifies the photographed X-48C deployment as post-landing drag/speed reduction, not as an observed yaw command.

3. Explain the force and moment. The generic yaw-control use is differential drag: more drag on one outboard side creates a yaw moment toward that side. Opening both sides similarly can provide braking. Do not assume that lift or roll coupling is exactly zero.

4. Follow the structural load. Each half has its own pressure load and hinge reaction; both hinge and actuation branches enter the local wing structure.

Quick check: How would a symmetric braking deployment differ from the asymmetric deployment needed for a drag-based yaw command?

5. Differential thrust — change magnitude, not nozzle direction

Photo reading: Centurion research UAV. Follow the row of propellers along the span; the motor forces act at different lateral distances from the CG.

NASA; EC98-44803-115. Photo source / credit. NASA Centurion control description.

1. Locate the hardware. Locate an outboard motor on each half-wing. The important geometry is the lateral separation of the thrust lines, not simply the number of motors.

2. Describe the change. The motors remain in place while their commanded power changes. NASA documents turns and yaw control on Centurion through differential power on outboard motors. A still photo does not reveal the power settings.

3. Explain the force and moment. For forward thrust, the body-axis yaw contribution is Mz = −yFx. More forward thrust on the right alone tends to yaw the nose left. Compare that sign with additional right-wing drag in the preceding card.

4. Follow the structural load. Propeller thrust and motor torque → motor mounts → supporting wing structure. A motor is a structural load input, not merely an electrical component.

Quick check: If all thrust lines passed through the CG, would unequal thrust necessarily produce the same yaw moment?

6. Thrust vectoring — turn the jet, obtain a reaction

Photo reading: F-18 HARV ground test, 1991. The metal vanes surrounding the exhaust are visible; the luminous plume is not an aerodynamic control surface.

NASA; EC91-0075-33. Photo source / credit. NASA HARV hardware description.

1. Locate the hardware. Find the hot exhaust exit and the paddle-like hardware around it. This is a real research-aircraft test, not a drawing of a generic tilting nozzle.

2. Describe the change. HARV used external vanes that could enter the exhaust stream. Other vectoring systems swivel a nozzle or tilt a propulsion unit; do not attribute those different mechanisms to this photograph.

3. Explain the force and moment. Changing exhaust momentum direction produces a reaction on the aircraft. The control moment depends on that force and its lever arm from the CG. Distinguish exhaust direction from the opposite reaction-force direction.

4. Follow the structural load. Hot-gas forces → vanes and actuator attachments → supporting aft-airframe structure; propulsion reactions also reach the engine mounts. Heat and vibration accompany these mechanical loads.

Quick check: What changed here that did not change in the differential-thrust example?

7. Circulation control — change the flow through slots

Photo reading: AMELIA circulation-control wind-tunnel model. The white smoke is a flow tracer from the wand, not a photograph of the pressurized slot jet itself.

NASA / Dominic Hart; ACD12-0003-019. Photo source / credit. NASA AMELIA blowing description.

1. Locate the hardware. Follow the smoke over the wing and locate the leading/trailing-edge regions. NASA describes narrow blowing slots along these edges; their small openings are not individually resolved in this whole-model view.

2. Describe the change. The controlled quantity is supplied airflow. Jets through slots alter the surrounding flow and wing loading. The mechanism does not require a new external panel deflection for each change in blowing.

3. Explain the force and moment. A left/right difference in aerodynamic force can generate a control moment. This AMELIA photograph demonstrates a research test setup, not a measured UAV roll command; see 7A-13 for the MAGMA flight-control example.

4. Follow the structural load. Aerodynamic force enters the wing; supply pressure and jet reactions load slot lips, internal ducts and their supports. A fixed exterior does not mean zero actuator-system or structural loads.

Quick check: Why should you not label the visible smoke wand as the aircraft’s circulation-control actuator?

8. Fly-by-wire and control allocation — the command layer

Photo reading: F-8 digital fly-by-wire electronics, 1971. Open bays reveal physical computing equipment and wiring, rather than another moving aerodynamic surface.

NASA; E-24741. Photo source / credit. NASA F-8 digital fly-by-wire archive.

1. Locate the hardware. Locate the open equipment bays. This historical installation makes the command-processing hardware visible; it is not a diagram of a modern UAV allocator.

2. Describe the change. Fly-by-wire transmits and processes electrical commands. Control allocation distributes requested moments among available physical effectors. Feedback can update commands using measured aircraft motion; these related functions are not identical.

3. Explain the force and moment. A computer does not create an aircraft control force directly. A commanded servo, motor or airflow valve must change a physical force. Surface limits can prevent the requested moment from being achieved.

4. Follow the structural load. Equipment trays carry avionics mass/vibration loads. The large control reactions still enter through the commanded surfaces, motors, hinges and mounts—not through the software.

Quick check: Trace one complete chain: roll request → mixer → two actuators → elevon motion → aerodynamic forces → wing reactions.

Three distinctions to keep: spoiler versus asymmetric spoileron use; differential thrust magnitude versus vectored thrust direction; and a physical effector versus the software that commands it. Use the body-axis cross product in 7A-3b whenever a moment sign is uncertain.
