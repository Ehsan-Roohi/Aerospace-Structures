Multi-Objective Design: Pareto Fronts with NSGA-II

pareto-nsga2 0.1.1
Scope. When mass and stiffness compete there is no single best design, only a set of trade-offs called the Pareto front. This page computes that front with NSGA-II (Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II, Deb et al. 2002) for two small structural problems: a beam section (Euler-Bernoulli beam, linear elastic, small deflection, exact closed forms) and a Warren truss (2D pin-jointed bars, linear elastic direct stiffness). Buckling of truss members is an Euler screen with effective length factor K = 1 and a solid square section; local wall buckling of thin tubes and boxes, shear deflection, fatigue and stability are not modelled. Every design shown as feasible is re-solved from scratch with the exact solver and all constraints are re-checked. The material cards are educational values, not certified allowables, and this page is independent of the Wing AeroStruct solver.

How it works

Dominance and the Pareto front
Both objectives are minimized: mass in grams and deflection in millimetres. Design A dominates design B when A is no worse in both objectives and strictly better in at least one. A design that no feasible design dominates is non-dominated (Pareto optimal); the set of all such designs is the Pareto front. On the plot, the region to the upper right of a point (heavier and more flexible) is the region it dominates: switch on the shading to see it for the selected design.
NSGA-II in five steps
  1. Initial population. N designs drawn uniformly inside the variable bounds with a seeded pseudo-random number generator, so the same seed always gives the same result.
  2. Non-dominated sorting. The population is peeled into fronts: front 1 is the set of designs nobody dominates, front 2 those dominated only by front 1, and so on. Constraints use Deb's rule: a feasible design always beats an infeasible one, two infeasible designs are compared by their total violation, two feasible designs by dominance.
  3. Crowding distance. Inside a front each design is scored by the normalized size of the box spanned by its two neighbours; the extreme designs get infinite distance. Larger distance means a less crowded part of the front and is preferred, which keeps the front spread out.
  4. Offspring. Parents are chosen by binary tournaments (feasibility, then violation, then rank, then crowding distance), recombined with simulated binary crossover (SBX, index eta_c, probability p_c) and perturbed by polynomial mutation (index eta_m, probability 1/n per variable).
  5. Elitist survival. Parents and offspring are pooled (2N designs), sorted again, and the best N survive; the last front that does not fit completely is truncated by crowding distance.
The hypervolume is the area of the objective plane dominated by the current front and bounded by a reference point (1.1 times the largest value of each objective over the feasible members of the initial population). It grows when the front moves towards the origin or spreads out, so a flat curve means convergence.
Beam problem and closed forms
Variables: width and depth (solid rectangle), outer diameter and wall (tube) or depth and wall with b = 0.6 h (box). Objectives: mass m = A L rho / 1000 and maximum deflection from the exact Euler-Bernoulli formulas: cantilever with tip load M = P L, v = P L^3 / (3 E I); cantilever with uniform load M = w L^2 / 2, v = w L^4 / (8 E I); simply supported midspan load M = P L / 4, v = P L^3 / (48 E I); simply supported uniform load M = w L^2 / 8, v = 5 w L^4 / (384 E I). Constraints: bending stress utilization U = sigma_max FS / sigma_allow <= 1 with sigma_max = M c / I, minimum wall or dimension t_min, and for hollow sections an open bore (D > 2 t, or b > 2 t for the box). Sign convention: y up, loads act downward, the deflection shown is the magnitude. Verification re-solves each displayed design with an independent finite element model (16 Hermite beam elements) and compares it with the closed form.

Classical result. At fixed mass (fixed area A) the second moment of area grows with the outer dimension: for the rectangle I = b h^3 / 12 = A h^2 / 12, and for the tube I = (A / 16) (D^2 + d^2), both increasing in the outer size. So every Pareto optimal section uses the largest depth or diameter allowed, except at the light end where the wall (or width) has reached its lower bound and only the outer dimension can shrink. The front is therefore the union of two boundary segments, closed by the stress constraint at the light end. This closed-form front is drawn as the thin black line; the grid sweep and NSGA-II should land on it (the agreement tile reports the mean normalized gap).

Warren truss problem
The shared 6-panel Warren truss (span 6000 mm, pinned left, roller right, 2000 N down at each interior bottom node) is solved with the 2D direct stiffness method: member force N = (E A / L) du_axial, stress N / A, mass sum(A L rho) / 1000. Variables: the chord area, the diagonal area and optionally the height h, which moves every top chord node. Objectives: mass and the downward deflection of the midspan bottom node (node 3). Constraints for every member: stress utilization |N| FS / (A sigma_allow) <= 1 and, for members in compression, the Euler screen |N| FS / P_cr <= 1 with P_cr = pi^2 E I / (K L)^2, I = A^2 / 12 (solid square bar) and K = 1. Because this truss is statically determinate, the member forces do not depend on the areas; only the deflections do, which makes the mass versus stiffness trade-off very clean. A singular stiffness matrix is reported as an unstable structure instead of numbers.
Limitations
Linear elastic models only; no local buckling of thin walls, no shear deformation, no fatigue, no dynamic loads. The Euler buckling check is a screening indicator with an assumed effective length. The trade-off readings ("lighter by", "stiffer by") are interpolated along the computed front, so they are plot readings, not verified designs; the nearest front design is verified when you select it. NSGA-II is a population heuristic: the front it returns is an approximation that improves with generations, which is why the exact reference sweep is shown for 2-variable problems. References: Deb, Pratap, Agarwal and Meyarivan, IEEE Trans. Evol. Comput. 6 (2002); Deb and Agrawal, Complex Systems 9 (1995) for SBX; Zitzler and Thiele (1999) for the hypervolume; Gere and Timoshenko, Mechanics of Materials, for the beam formulas.
