Truss Member Sizing: Fully Stressed and Gradient Methods

truss-sizing 0.1.1
Scope. Two-dimensional pin-jointed truss, linear elastic, small displacements, solved by the direct stiffness method. Members are sized under three checks: stress, |N| FS / (A sigma_allow) ≤ 1 where FS is the factor of safety; an Euler buckling screen for solid square sections, P_cr = pi^2 E (A^2/12) / (K L)^2 with an assumed effective length factor K (a screening indicator, not a stability analysis); and a limit on one nodal displacement. Tension-only bracing wires that come out in compression are flagged slack: the linear model is then not valid for that wire and the page reports it rather than iterating a tension-only solution. Material cards are representative educational values, not certified allowables. Nothing here is a strength, stability or fatigue certification, and the page is independent of the Wing AeroStruct solver. Every design shown below is re-solved from scratch with the exact solver before it is displayed.

How it works

Model and checks
The truss is solved by the direct stiffness method: the global stiffness matrix K is assembled from the bar stiffnesses E A / L, the supported degrees of freedom (DOF) are removed, and K u = F is solved by a Cholesky factorization. A zero pivot means a mechanism and is reported as an unstable structure. Member force N = (E A / L) (du along the axis), positive in tension; stress sigma = N / A. Utilization is U = |N| FS / (A sigma_allow), and for compression members of groups with a square section the buckling screen U_b = |N| FS / P_cr is also applied; the larger of the two governs. Mass is sum(A L rho) / 1000 in grams. Sign convention: y up, loads positive upward.
Fully stressed design (FSD)
The classical heuristic: resize every design variable so that its most loaded member reaches U = 1, A ← A U for stress and A ← A sqrt(U_b) for the buckling screen (P_cr grows with A^2), take the larger, never go below the minimum area, re-solve and repeat until max |U - 1| < 1e-3 for the variables that are not at their minimum, or 50 iterations. Slack wires carry no constraint and are sent to the minimum area. If the displacement limit is then violated, all areas are scaled by the same factor: member forces are unchanged by a uniform scaling and displacements scale as 1/factor, so the limit is met exactly. For statically determinate trusses with stress constraints the fully stressed design is the minimum-mass design (Razani 1965); for indeterminate trusses or with a displacement limit it generally is not.
Gradient method: sequential linear programming (SLP)
Analytical sensitivities: differentiating K u = F gives du/dA_j = -K^-1 (dK/dA_j) u, one back-substitution per design variable with the already factored K, and dN_i/dA_j follows from the member force formula. The displacement sensitivity uses the adjoint method: one solve with a unit load at the constrained DOF gives lambda, and d(delta)/dA_j = -lambda^T (dK/dA_j) u for every variable at once. The tests check both against central finite differences.

Each iteration linearizes the mass and all constraints in the reciprocal variables y = 1/A (member stress is exactly linear in 1/A for a determinate truss, so this is far more accurate than linearizing in A), restricts the step to a move-limit box (an area may shrink by at most 20 percent and grow by at most 100 percent per iteration), and solves the resulting linear program (LP) with a small dense two-phase simplex method using Bland's rule. A variable whose step changes sign has its shrink limit halved; it recovers after two steps in the same direction. If the LP has no feasible point inside the box, the iteration falls back to one fully stressed repair step. The loop stops when the relative mass change is below 1e-4 at a design within 0.1 percent of feasibility, or after 80 iterations. The final design is then made strictly feasible by at most a few fully stressed polish steps plus uniform scaling for the displacement limit, and re-verified.
Catalog rounding and verification
The lighter of the two continuous optima is rounded up, variable by variable, to the nearest stock size in its group catalog, then re-solved. The mass difference is the price of discreteness. Every design on this page (yours, FSD, gradient, catalog) is verified by a fresh solve: the utilizations, the governing constraint and the badges come from that re-solve, never from the optimizer's internal estimate.
Things to try
Lower the displacement limit of the Wright bay to 20 or 12 mm and compare the two optimizers: FSD can only scale everything up, while the gradient method thickens the members with the best deflection sensitivity per gram (the wires). Switch to member-by-member sizing to see under-used members drop to their minimum area, then check the design against the other load cases in the last table: a half-wing sized for lift alone is not safe in the landing or negative-g cases. Turn the buckling screen off on the Warren truss to see the compression chords collapse to the stress-only size.
Limitations and references
Linear elastic bars only: no joint stiffness, no member self-weight, no geometric nonlinearity, no fatigue, no certified allowables. The buckling check is a screen for a solid square section with an assumed effective length; real members need a proper stability analysis. Slack wires are reported, not removed: in a bay whose two crossed wires share one area, the linear model splits the shear equally between the tensioned and the slack wire, so the tensioned wire's force is under-estimated by up to a factor of two for that load case. One load case is sized at a time; the last table checks the result against the others. References: R. Razani, "Behavior of fully stressed design of structures and its relationship to minimum-weight design", AIAA Journal 3 (1965); L. A. Schmit, "Structural design by systematic synthesis", 2nd ASCE Conference on Electronic Computation (1960); C. Fleury and V. Braibant, "Structural optimization: a new dual method using mixed variables", Int. J. Numer. Methods Eng. 23 (1986); R. T. Haftka and Z. Gurdal, Elements of Structural Optimization (1992); U. Kirsch, Structural Optimization: Fundamentals and Applications (1993).
