See bending and torsion

These enlarged schematics sit beside the aircraft model to separate two deformation mechanisms. Dashed grey is the original shape; blue is bending; red is twisting. The aircraft mesh above is not deformed by this demonstration.

Bending: the spanwise centreline curves and the tip moves upward; under the illustrated positive bending, upper members are compressed and lower members are stretched. Torsion: an airfoil section rotates about a spanwise axis, changing its local incidence. Bending slope is not the same as twist.

Teaching cases: a uniform-load, constant-EI cantilever for bending, and a constant-GJ cantilever with a tip torque for torsion. The real D.520 is tapered and has distributed loads and variable stiffness, so its numerical shapes differ. The animation prescribes a slow load cycle: it is not a natural mode, flutter, or a computed D.520 response.

For computed deformation, open D.520 Wing Lab, choose Diagrams for numerical deflection and twist, or Modes for the structural mode shapes. Use the viewport's Deformed switch to compare with the original geometry.

From the aircraft model to structural mechanics

This is a geometric reconstruction with movable controls, not a flight or stress simulation. Moving a slider rotates a part to illustrate its location and travel; it does not compute pressure, lift, stability or structural stress.

First identify the parts

Ailerons are near the outboard trailing edges: differential deflection changes the left/right aerodynamic loading and commands roll. Elevators on the horizontal tail change tail loading and command pitch. The rudder on the vertical tail changes side force and commands yaw. Flaps are inboard trailing-edge devices that change lift and drag. Control force enters the airframe through hinges and supporting members; these local loads are not solved by the animation.

Why the wing needs an internal structure

Pressure acts over the skin. Ribs preserve the airfoil shape and transfer local loading; spar caps and stressed skin form tension–compression load paths for bending; webs and connected skins carry shear and torque; stringers stiffen the skin against local buckling. A smooth external surface hides these members, so the whole-aircraft view alone cannot show their stresses.

Continue with the numerical wing lesson

Open the D.520 structural workspace, then choose Theory · guided lesson. Follow the sequence pressure → net span load → shear/moment/torque → section stresses → deformation → stability. Use Section for the internal cross-section and Loads for the actual prescribed load distribution.

Before running: predict what increasing the load factor from 1 to 6 will do to stress and deflection. Then predict whether thicker skin or larger spar caps will be more effective for bending and twisting. Check both the result and the added mass. All strength conclusions depend on the assumed gauges, material properties and boundary conditions; this is not an airworthiness assessment.

One rule: every dimension has a source

Where each number comes from

Printed values come straight from the manual's tables or plates. Scaled values were measured on Pl. 0, which is drawn at 1/50 and scanned at 150 dpi, so 1 m is 118 pixels. Derived values are computed from those, and guesses are labelled as guesses. When two sources disagreed, a third one decided.
