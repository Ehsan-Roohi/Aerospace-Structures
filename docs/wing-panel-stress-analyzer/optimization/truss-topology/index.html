Ground-Structure Topology Optimization

truss-topology 0.1.1
Scope. Instead of sizing a truss you drew, the computer starts from a dense ground structure of candidate pin-jointed bars on a rectangular grid and decides which bars should exist. Model: 2D linear elastic truss (direct stiffness method), one static load case, continuous member areas, no self weight. Two deterministic optimizers are compared: optimality criteria (OC) for minimum compliance at a fixed material volume, and minimum-volume plastic design solved as a linear program (LP). Every layout shown is rebuilt from the kept bars and re-solved from scratch with the exact elastic solver; the Euler buckling column is a screening indicator only (assumed solid square section, effective length factor K = 1). No joint design, no stability or fatigue claims, no certified allowables; independent of the Wing AeroStruct solver.

How it works

Ground structure
The nodes of an nx by ny grid are joined by every candidate bar whose grid index differences are both at most r. A candidate that passes exactly through an intermediate grid node (for example two steps along a row) is skipped, because the same force path already exists as a chain of shorter bars. Supports and the load are placed on grid nodes. The baseline, your own design point, is this ground structure with every bar at the reference area A_ref.
OC: minimum compliance at fixed volume
Compliance is the work of the load, C = f · u, a measure of flexibility. The optimality criteria (OC) method (Bendsoe and Sigmund 2003) adjusts the member areas so that the compliance is minimal for a fixed total volume V_t = volfrac × A_ref × sum of candidate lengths. The sensitivity of compliance to a bar area is dC/dA_i = -N_i^2 L_i / (E A_i^2) = -sigma_i^2 L_i / E: bars with a high stress are worth more material. Each iteration multiplies every area by (its sensitivity divided by lambda times its length) to the power 0.5 (damping), moves at most 20 percent, respects the floor and ceiling, and finds the Lagrange multiplier lambda by bisection so that the volume stays exactly at V_t. A safeguard halves the move if a step would raise the compliance. Iterations stop when the largest area change is below the tolerance (relative to the largest area) or after the maximum number of iterations. At the optimum every bar that is neither at its floor nor at its ceiling carries the same stress magnitude sqrt(lambda E), the fully stressed condition.
LP: minimum-volume plastic design
Dorn, Gomory and Greenberg (1964) wrote the minimum-volume truss on a ground structure as a linear program (LP). Each candidate bar gets two variables, a tension force t_i and a compression force c_i, both nonnegative. Equilibrium at every free degree of freedom (dof) reads B (t - c) = f, where B holds the direction cosines of the bars. A fully stressed bar needs the area A_i = t_i / sigma_t + c_i / sigma_c, so the volume sum L_i (t_i / sigma_t + c_i / sigma_c) is linear in the variables and is minimized. The page solves this with its own dense two-phase simplex (no libraries): phase 1 finds a feasible equilibrium with artificial variables, phase 2 reduces the volume. Bland's rule is implemented and used as the anti-cycling fallback; the default entering rule is Dantzig's most negative reduced cost with a lexicographic ratio test, because pure Bland's rule needs tens of thousands of degenerate pivots on these problems (every unloaded free dof gives a zero right-hand side). The optimum is a vertex, so at most as many bars as free dofs are nonzero: the layout is statically determinate. With a single load case and a grid that gets finer, the LP solution approaches Michell's (1904) least-volume structures.
Why the two volumes differ, and the classical identity
OC solves an elastic problem: the stiffest truss for a volume budget, with no stress limit. The LP solves a plastic design: the lightest truss whose bars are all at the allowable, with no stiffness requirement and no compatibility of deformations. For one load case with equal allowables in tension and compression the two problems share the same optimal layout (Michell, Hemp 1973), and the areas differ only by a common factor. Then C × V × E = (sigma_allow × V_LP)^2 for the optimal layout at any volume V. The page reports this product for the OC layout (it can only be above 1, the Michell lower bound on compliance) and for the LP layout (exactly 1 when it is fully stressed). The LP volume is the minimum for any layout that respects the stress limit, so the OC layout scaled up or down to put its worst bar at the allowable can never be lighter than the LP design. A compliance within a fraction of a percent of the bound can still show a visibly different layout: compliance is flat near the optimum while slowly vanishing bars are still shrinking, so tighten the OC tolerance to see the layouts converge.
Verification, thresholds and mechanisms
Bars below theta times the largest area are hidden. The remaining bars (a dangling bar at an unloaded joint is removed, two collinear bars at an unloaded two-bar joint become one continuous bar) are re-solved exactly with the direct stiffness method, which reports member forces, stresses, utilization against the allowables, mass and compliance. If the thresholded layout is kinematically unstable the page says so and tries a lower threshold automatically. A plastic design can be a mechanism even with every nonzero bar kept, typically a roller hanging on one vertical bar, because equilibrium under the given load needs no horizontal reaction. In that case the load does no work in the zero-stiffness mode, the member forces are still unique and are reported, the mode is drawn in purple, and the layout is flagged as not usable without bracing. Mass is sum A_i L_i × rho / 1000 in grams. The baseline and the OC layout are also reported scaled so that their worst bar is exactly at the allowable; scaling all areas by one factor does not change the force distribution in a linear truss.
References and limitations
A. G. M. Michell, The limits of economy of material in frame-structures, Philosophical Magazine 8 (1904) 589-597. W. S. Dorn, R. E. Gomory and H. J. Greenberg, Automatic design of optimal structures, Journal de Mecanique 3 (1964) 25-52. W. S. Hemp, Optimum Structures, Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1973. M. P. Bendsoe and O. Sigmund, Topology Optimization: Theory, Methods and Applications, Springer, 2003 (optimality criteria, truss topology design). Limitations: pin-jointed bars with continuous areas and one static load case; no buckling constraint (the Euler column is a screen only), no joint or connection design, no self weight, no dynamic or fatigue loads, no certified material allowables. Candidate bars may cross without a joint; a real structure needs a decision at each crossing.
